# ex 3 pg 275
Show that $7$ is an eigenvalue of a
$$
A\vec{w}=7\vec{w}
$$
Im guessing that we do this:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A|7c
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Nope:
$$
\begin{align}
A\vec{w}=7\vec{w} \\
A\vec{w}-7\vec{w}=0 \\
(A-7I)\vec{w}=0
\end{align}
$$
$$
\left(   
\begin{bmatrix}
3  & -2 \\
1 & 0
\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}
7 & 0 \\
0 & 7
\end{bmatrix}
\right)\vec{w}=0
$$
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
-6 & 6 \\
5 & -5
\end{bmatrix}\vec{w}=0
$$
And now it is in a form where we can solve with augmented matrix
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
-6 & 6 &| &  0 \\
5 & -5 & | & 0
\end{bmatrix} \sim 
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & | & 0 \\
0 & 0 & | & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
\implies \vec{x}=\begin{bmatrix}
x_{2} \\
x_{2}
\end{bmatrix} =x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$
So the eigenvector is $$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$.
In fact, any vector in that form will have the same eigenvalue.

We can really easily find eigen values from triangular matrices because their diagonal elements are eigenvalues.

Consider where we solve for an arbitrary eigenvalue $\lambda$. We would first do the matrix subtraction and get:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a_{11}-\lambda & a_{12} & a_{13} \\
0 & a_{22}-\lambda & a_{23} \\
0 & 0 & a_{33}-\lambda
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**In order for $\lambda$ to be an eigenvalue then the solution to this equation must be non-trivial.**  WE need a free variable.
$\implies\lambda=$ $a_{11} \lor a_{22} \lor a_{33}$![[meta/images/Pasted image 20250310171924.png]]
